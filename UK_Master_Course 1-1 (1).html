Module 1 · Part 1 of 12 · ~3 hours

UK Compliance Landscape — Who Regulates What, and Why

Learning Objectives

  • Name the seven UK regulators and supervisory bodies an accountant interacts with on a daily basis and describe each one's statutory remit.
  • Distinguish HMRC's role (tax collection, AML supervision of unsupervised agents) from Companies House's role (registry of companies, public record of accounts).
  • Explain the role of the Financial Reporting Council (FRC), its sub-bodies, and the standards it sets.
  • List the recognised professional bodies for accountants and bookkeepers and the AML supervision each performs.
  • Map a typical client engagement to the regulators that will receive filings.
  • Identify when an accountant becomes “in scope” for AML supervision and the consequences of operating without it.

1.1 Why “compliance” is the centre of gravity for UK accountants

The UK does not have a single Inland Revenue and Companies Office; it has a layered system of regulators, each with statutory powers, distinct deadlines, and distinct penalty regimes. As an accountant you do not simply “keep books” — you operate as the bridge between your client and these regulators. Failure to meet a deadline triggers automatic penalties, often on a daily basis, and serious failures can result in director disqualification, professional sanction, or criminal prosecution.

Compliance has three operational pillars:

  1. Record-keeping — capturing every business transaction in a form that satisfies HMRC's and (for companies) Companies Act record requirements.
  2. Reporting — transforming those records into the statutory returns and accounts each regulator requires, on the deadline they set.
  3. Paying — settling liabilities (Income Tax, Corporation Tax, VAT, NI, PAYE, etc.) by the legally specified date.

The technical work of bookkeeping (debits and credits) is in service of these three pillars. A trial balance is not an end in itself; it is the input to a CT600, an SA100 or a set of FRS 102 accounts. From day one of this course, every ledger entry should be evaluated by the question: “Where will this end up on the statutory return, and is it correctly classified for that purpose?”

1.2 HMRC — His Majesty's Revenue and Customs

HMRC is the UK's tax authority, formed in 2005 by the merger of Inland Revenue and HM Customs & Excise. It sits as a non-ministerial department of government, responsible to Parliament via the Treasury but not directly part of any minister's portfolio. This independence matters: Parliament sets the law (the Taxes Acts); HMRC administers it.

1.2.3 The Government Gateway and Agent Services Account (ASA)

Every individual and business that interacts digitally with HMRC needs a Government Gateway account. As an agent you will hold:

  • A personal Government Gateway for your own Self Assessment.
  • An Agent Online Services Account — the “old” account — used for filing SA100, CT600 and managing 64-8 authorisations.
  • An Agent Services Account (ASA) — the “new” account, mandatory for MTD VAT, MTD ITSA, Trust Registration Service, and CGT-on-property reporting.

You authorise an agent relationship by either: (a) the client signing form 64-8 (legacy method, for SA, CT, PAYE, VAT pre-MTD); or (b) the digital authorisation request via the ASA, where the client receives a code from HMRC that they pass to you to enter in your ASA. The digital authorisation has overtaken paper 64-8s for most live filing services.

1.3 Companies House — The Registry

Companies House (CH) is the registrar of companies in the UK. It is an executive agency of the Department for Business and Trade. Crucially, Companies House is a registry, not a regulator: it records what is filed but historically does not validate the truthfulness of filings beyond formal completeness. The Economic Crime & Corporate Transparency Act 2023 (ECCTA) is now changing that — CH gains identity-verification, query and rejection powers in phased rollouts from 2024 to 2027.

ECCTA reforms — be aware

The Economic Crime & Corporate Transparency Act 2023 introduces:

  • Mandatory identity verification (IDV) for directors, PSCs and people filing on behalf of companies.
  • Removal of the option to file abridged accounts (only filleted accounts will remain — full accounts must be prepared even if some pages aren't filed publicly).

  • Enhanced CH powers to query, reject and remove incorrect information.

Rollout is staged 2024–2027. Always check the latest CH transition timetable when advising clients on first-year filings.

1.4 The Financial Reporting Council (FRC) and its successor ARGA

The FRC sets UK accounting standards (FRS 100 to FRS 105), audit standards (ISAs UK), corporate governance code, and the actuarial standards. It is in the process of being replaced by the Audit, Reporting and Governance Authority (ARGA) following the post-Carillion Kingman Review. ARGA will have stronger statutory powers including direct sanction of company directors over financial reporting failures.

For day-to-day bookkeeping the FRC matters because it owns the financial reporting standards you must apply when preparing accounts:

  • FRS 100 — framework: which standard applies to which entity.
  • FRS 101 — reduced disclosure for subsidiaries of IFRS groups.
  • FRS 102 — the main UK GAAP standard, most clients you will work on.
  • FRS 102 Section 1A — reduced disclosures for small entities (qualifying as small under Companies Act).
  • FRS 103 — insurance contracts (specialist).
  • FRS 104 — interim financial reporting.
  • FRS 105 — the micro-entities regime: simplest, fewest disclosures, no fair value model.

We will spend modules 29 to 32 on FRS 105, FRS 102 Section 1A and FRS 102 in granular detail, including why each disclosure is required by the standard.

1.6 The Office for Professional Body AML Supervision (OPBAS)

OPBAS sits within the FCA and supervises the AML supervisors. It does not directly supervise accountants, but it ensures the professional bodies do their AML supervisory job consistently.

1.7 Anti-Money Laundering (AML) supervision — you must be supervised

Critical legal point

Under the Money Laundering Regulations 2017 (as amended), any person providing accountancy services or tax services by way of business must be supervised for AML — either by their professional body or, if not in a body, directly by HMRC. Operating without supervision is a criminal offence carrying up to two years' imprisonment (MLR 2017, reg.56).

HMRC's AML registration costs around £300 per premises per year (2024/25) plus a £40 registration fee and a £150 fit-and-proper test for senior staff. If you are AAT, ACCA, ICAEW etc. you do not additionally register with HMRC for AML — your body covers it. We cover AML in depth in Module 83.

1.8 Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) — data protection

If your business processes personal data (and as an accountant you process names, NINOs, salaries, bank details, addresses), you must register with the ICO (a notification fee, currently £40–£2,900 a year depending on size) and comply with the UK GDPR & Data Protection Act 2018. Module 86 covers this in detail.

1.9 The Pensions Regulator (TPR)

TPR enforces auto-enrolment compliance. Every employer (regardless of size) must auto-enrol eligible jobholders into a qualifying pension scheme, contribute the statutory minimum, and file an annual re-declaration of compliance. Penalties for non-compliance start at £400 fixed and escalate to £10,000/day for the largest employers (Module 23).

1.10 The Charity Commission & OSCR

Charities in England & Wales register with the Charity Commission for England & Wales (CCEW) once income exceeds £5,000 (or any income for CIOs). Scotland uses OSCR; Northern Ireland uses the Charity Commission for Northern Ireland (CCNI). Charity accounts follow the Charities SORP — the Statement of Recommended Practice based on FRS 102 — with extensive narrative disclosures (Trustees' Annual Report). Module 71 covers charity conversion in depth.

1.11 Insolvency Service

Part of the Department for Business and Trade. Investigates bankruptcies, supervises liquidators, oversees director disqualifications, and runs the Bankruptcy Restrictions / Director Disqualification regimes. We meet the Insolvency Service in modules 60–64 (cessation routes).

Module 1 — Summary

  • HMRC administers tax (Income Tax, CT, VAT, NI, CGT, IHT). Authority comes from Parliament via the Taxes Acts; HMRC writes guidance but only Parliament makes law.
  • Companies House is a registry of companies and LLPs. ECCTA 2023 is currently turning it into a more active gatekeeper with identity verification and query powers.
  • FRC sets accounting and audit standards (FRS 100–105). Being replaced by ARGA with stronger statutory powers.
  • Accountants must be supervised for AML — by their professional body (ICAEW, ACCA, AAT, etc.) or directly by HMRC. Unsupervised practice is a criminal offence.
  • Other regulators in the orbit: TPR (pensions), ICO (data protection), Charity Commission/OSCR/CCNI, Insolvency Service.
  • A typical small-company client triggers a dozen distinct annual compliance points spread across at least four regulators.

Module 1 — End-of-Section MCQ (10 questions)

Click the option you believe is correct. Feedback appears immediately. Aim for 8/10.

Q1
Which of the following is not administered by HMRC?
Strike-off (DS01) is processed by Companies House. HMRC may object to a strike-off if tax is owed, but it does not administer the process.
Q2
A new private limited company is incorporated on 14 March 2024. When are its first statutory accounts due at Companies House?
First accounts of a private company are due 21 months after incorporation. Subsequent accounts are due 9 months after the accounting reference date (ARD).
Q3
Which standard is most commonly applied to small UK private companies that do not qualify as micro-entities?
FRS 102 Section 1A reduces disclosures for entities qualifying as small under the Companies Act size limits while still applying FRS 102 recognition and measurement.
Q4
Operating as a paid bookkeeper without AML supervision is:
MLR 2017 reg.56 makes unsupervised practice a criminal offence punishable by up to 2 years' imprisonment.
Q5
The Confirmation Statement (CS01) is filed at:
CS01 confirms the registered office, officers, shareholders, PSC and SIC code. Filing fee £34 (digital) from May 2024.
Q6
A “BX9” HMRC postcode refers to:
BX9 is a non-geographic mail routing code. Correspondence is sub-routed by the address line (e.g. “Self Assessment”, “Corporation Tax Services”, “PT Operations North East England”).
Q7
Which body is the AML supervisor for AAT-qualified bookkeepers in independent practice?
AAT is a recognised AML supervisory body and supervises members in practice. Members do not also need HMRC supervision.
Q8
A fixed charge over a company's premises is created on 1 February. By when must it be registered at Companies House under the standard rule?
21 days — CA 2006 s.859A. Out of time, the charge is void against a liquidator and the company would need a court order to register late.
Q9
Which of the following is a non-ministerial department?
HMRC is a non-ministerial department reporting to Parliament via the Treasury, established by CRCA 2005. The others are executive agencies or arm's-length bodies.
Q10
Which Act is replacing the FRC with a stronger regulator (ARGA)?
The Audit Reform Bill (post-Kingman) creates ARGA. As of 2024/25 the FRC continues to operate while transition occurs.

2.2 Sole trader — the legal nuts and bolts

A sole trader (or “self-employed individual”) is a person who carries on a trade, profession or vocation in their own name. There is no legal separation between the person and the business. Their assets and the “business” assets are the same in law — if the business goes bankrupt, the personal assets follow.

2.2.1 Registering as a sole trader

You must register for Self Assessment with HMRC by 5 October following the end of the tax year in which you started self-employment. Registration is online at gov.uk/register-for-self-assessment; HMRC issues a UTR (Unique Taxpayer Reference) within ~10 working days, and a separate Government Gateway login.

2.2.2 Tax footprint of a sole trader

Profits from self-employment are charged to:

  • Income Tax on profits above the Personal Allowance (£12,570 for 2024/25): 20% basic, 40% higher, 45% additional. Personal Allowance tapers away at £1 for every £2 of income above £100,000 — fully eroded at £125,140.
  • Class 4 NI: 6% on profits between £12,570 and £50,270; 2% above. (Reduced from 9% to 6% from 6 April 2024 in the Spring Budget; check current year for 2025/26.)
  • Class 2 NI: abolished as a flat-rate weekly charge from 6 April 2024. Voluntary Class 2 contributions remain available for self-employed earning below the Small Profits Threshold (currently £6,725) who wish to maintain a state pension contribution year — £3.45/week (2024/25 rate).

2.3 General Partnership

Two or more persons (individuals or corporates) carrying on business in common with a view to profit (Partnership Act 1890 s.1). No legal personality in England & Wales (the partnership is not a separate person); each partner is jointly and severally liable for partnership debts.

2.3.1 The partnership agreement

The PA 1890 provides default rules — equal sharing of profits, equal contribution to capital, no entitlement to remuneration for working in the business, etc. Almost all clients will want to override these by a written Partnership Agreement covering:

  • Profit-sharing ratio (PSR) and changes in PSR
  • Capital contributions and interest on capital
  • Salaries to partners (a P&L charge in commercial accounts but added back for tax)
  • Drawings limits and policies
  • Admission of new partners and retirement of existing partners
  • Dispute resolution
  • Continuation/dissolution on death of a partner

2.3.2 Tax for a general partnership

The partnership itself does not pay tax. It files an SA800 Partnership Tax Return that reports the partnership's profit; that profit is then allocated among the partners according to their PSR for the period and each partner reports their share on form SA104 (partnership pages) within their personal SA100.

2.3.3 Limited Partnership (LP) — specialist structure

An LP is a partnership with at least one general partner (unlimited liability) and one or more limited partners (limited to capital contribution). LPs are registered at Companies House under the Limited Partnerships Act 1907. Mostly used by private equity / VC funds. Not to be confused with LLP (next).

2.4 Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)

Created by the Limited Liability Partnerships Act 2000. An LLP is a separate legal entity (like a company) but is taxed like a partnership (transparent, members pay IT & NI on their share). Designed for professional firms (law, accountancy) that wanted limited liability without becoming companies.

2.4.1 Key LLP attributes

  • Minimum 2 members; minimum 2 designated members (with statutory filing duties).
  • No share capital; capital contributions are accounted for as members' loans/equity per the LLP agreement.
  • Files annual accounts at Companies House (small/micro filing exemptions broadly mirror companies).
  • Files a confirmation statement annually.
  • Members are taxed personally on their share of LLP profits as if they were partners (ITTOIA 2005 s.863).
  • Salaried members rule (ITA 2007 s.863A) deems certain “disguised employees” in LLPs to be employees for PAYE/NI — you must apply the three-condition test annually.

2.5 Private Limited Company (Ltd)

The workhorse of UK business. Roughly 4.6 million companies on the register. The company is a separate legal person under CA 2006 s.16. Shareholders own; directors manage; their roles and the relationship between them are set out in the company's Articles of Association and (often) a separate Shareholders' Agreement.

2.5.1 Constitutional documents

  • Memorandum of Association — a now-very-short document signed by initial subscribers confirming intention to form a company. Replaced extensively in 2009 by simpler form.
  • Articles of Association — the company's internal rulebook. The Model Articles for private companies (Companies (Model Articles) Regulations 2008, Sch 1) are the default; can be modified or replaced by bespoke Articles.
  • Shareholders' Agreement — private contract between shareholders. Often covers pre-emption, drag/tag rights, exit mechanics, valuation. Not filed at Companies House (unlike Articles, which are public).

2.5.2 Tax footprint of a Ltd

Two layers of tax:

  1. Corporation Tax on company profits — 25% main rate above £250,000; 19% small profits rate up to £50,000; marginal relief in between (effective marginal rate ~26.5%).
  2. Income Tax on amounts extracted — salary (taxed under PAYE with Class 1 NI primary 8% / secondary 13.8%), or dividends (taxed at 8.75% basic / 33.75% higher / 39.35% additional, with a £500 dividend allowance for 2024/25, reducing to £500 in 2024/25 from £1,000 in 2023/24).

2.5.3 The salary/dividend mix — the classic owner-managed company question

For a director-shareholder, the optimal extraction structure depends on (a) whether they need NI credits, (b) the company's profit level, (c) their other income, and (d) personal allowance interactions. The traditional structure for a single-director company in 2024/25:

  • Pay a salary equal to the Secondary NI Threshold (£9,100 for 2024/25, raised to £5,000 for 2025/26 with Employer NI changes from April 2025) to maintain a qualifying year for the State Pension without triggering Employer or Employee NI.
  • Take the rest as dividends out of post-tax retained profits.
2025/26 watch — Employer NI changes

From 6 April 2025, the Secondary Threshold drops to £5,000 (from £9,100) and Employer NI rises to 15% (from 13.8%). The classic “£9,100 salary + dividends” strategy needs revisiting from 2025/26 — in many cases a higher salary (up to the Personal Allowance £12,570) becomes optimal because the Employer's Allowance (£10,500 from April 2025, where eligible) covers the resulting Employer NI, and the salary saves Corporation Tax at 19%–25%.

2.6 Public Limited Company (PLC)

A company whose name ends in “plc”. The label means it can offer shares to the public — not that it must, and not that it is listed. Distinct features:

  • Minimum allotted share capital £50,000, of which 25% must be paid up.
  • Minimum 2 directors (vs 1 for Ltd) and a company secretary (mandatory).
  • Accounts due 6 months after ARD (vs 9 months for Ltd).
  • Cannot file abridged or filleted accounts — full accounts required.
  • If listed (LSE Main, AIM, AQUIS) — FCA rules apply on top.

2.7 Community Interest Company (CIC)

A limited company (Ltd or company limited by guarantee) with a community/social-purpose lock. Created by the Companies (Audit, Investigations & Community Enterprise) Act 2004. Regulated by the CIC Regulator (a small office within Companies House). Files a CIC34 community-interest report annually with the accounts. Asset lock means assets cannot be distributed to members — if dissolved, must transfer to another asset-locked body.

2.8 Charity, Charitable Incorporated Organisation (CIO) and Charitable Company

Three principal forms:

  • Unincorporated charity (charitable trust or association) — trustees personally liable; no separate legal personality.
  • Charitable company limited by guarantee — legal personality; dual-registered (Companies House + Charity Commission).
  • CIO — Charitable Incorporated Organisation, created by Charities Act 2011 Part 11. Single regulator (Charity Commission) so no Companies House filing burden. Two flavours: Foundation CIO (members are the trustees) and Association CIO (separate membership body with trustees elected by members).

Charity-specific tax treatment: most income exempt under CTA 2010 Part 11 (trading income exemption) provided used for charitable purposes; non-primary-purpose trading sometimes routed through a wholly-owned trading subsidiary which gift-aids profit up to the parent. Bookkeeping Fundamentals — Double-Entry from First Principles

Learning Objectives

  • State and explain the accounting equation, including its rearrangements.
  • Apply the rules of double-entry to record assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses.
  • Post compound and reversing journals correctly.
  • Prepare a trial balance from a list of ledger balances and identify the types of error a TB will and will not detect.
  • Translate any business event into a journal using the “source → debit → credit → narrative” method.

3.1 What is bookkeeping?

Bookkeeping is the systematic recording of every financial transaction of a business, in chronological order, in a way that allows the business to: (a) know its financial position at any moment (balance sheet); (b) measure its performance over a period (P&L); (c) prove the figures to an external party (HMRC, Companies House, banks, investors); and (d) detect errors and fraud. The discipline is approximately 700 years old — codified by the Italian friar Luca Pacioli in Summa de Arithmetica (1494) — but the underlying logic has not changed since.

3.2 The accounting equation

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

This is the only equation in accounting. It is true after every single transaction, in every business, in every era. Variations:

  • Assets − Liabilities = Equity (net worth) — how the balance sheet is normally presented in the UK.
  • Equity = Capital introduced + Retained profits − Drawings/Dividends — expansion of the equity component over time.
  • Profit = Income − Expenses — measuring the change in equity from operations between two balance sheet dates (excluding capital introduced/withdrawn).

3.3 Double-entry — the symmetry rule

Every transaction has at least two equal and opposite entries: one debit and one credit (or several adding to balance). This guarantees the accounting equation remains true. The direction of debit/credit is fixed by the type of account:

3.5 Compound journals

Where a transaction needs more than two postings — e.g. payroll — we use a compound journal:

Three credits, two debits — total debits 10,900 = total credits 10,900. Always check.

3.6 The trial balance

A trial balance lists every ledger account balance with debits in one column and credits in another. The columns must total to the same figure. If they do, you have arithmetic integrity (one of the three checks below).

Errors a TB will detectErrors a TB will not detect
One-sided entries (only Dr or only Cr posted)Errors of omission (transaction not recorded at all)
Different amounts on Dr and CrErrors of commission (right side, wrong account)
Casting errors in a single ledgerErrors of principle (e.g. capital expenditure expensed as repair)
Transposition errors that change a side totalCompensating errors (two errors that cancel out)
Original entry errors (wrong amount on both sides)
Reversal errors (Dr and Cr swapped)

This is why the trial balance is a necessary but not sufficient check. The other two checks at month-end are: bank reconciliation (proves cash account against external bank statement) and control account reconciliations (proves debtor/creditor control accounts against the underlying sub-ledgers).

  • Accounting equation Assets = Liabilities + Equity holds after every transaction.
  • Double-entry: equal debits and credits per transaction. DEAD CLIC mnemonic.
  • Eight universal transaction patterns cover the bulk of bookkeeping.
  • Trial balance proves arithmetic but cannot detect errors of omission, commission, principle, compensation, original entry or reversal.

4.1 What is the chart of accounts (COA)?

The chart of accounts is the list of every account in the general ledger, organised by code or category. It is the backbone of the accounting system — everything that gets posted lands in one of these accounts. The COA structure determines how easily you can produce: a P&L (income/expense classification); a balance sheet (asset/liability/equity classification); a VAT return (VAT codes); and statutory accounts (mapped to the Companies Act formats).

4.3 Control accounts and sub-ledgers

The general ledger contains control accounts (single-line balances summarising sub-ledgers). Examples:

  • Trade Debtors Control — summary; the underlying sub-ledger has every customer's balance broken out (Aged Debtors Report).
  • Trade Creditors Control — summary; underlying = supplier balances (Aged Creditors).
  • VAT Control — summary; underlying = transaction-level VAT detail by tax code.
  • PAYE/NIC Control — summary; underlying = month-by-month payroll detail.

Control accounts must reconcile to the sub-ledger every period. If they don't, an entry has been posted directly to the control account bypassing the sub-ledger — common in a manual journal that should have been done as a customer credit note instead.

  • Identify the controls embedded at each step.

5.1 The cycle in 9 steps

  1. Capture source documents — collect every invoice, receipt, bank statement, expense claim, payroll record. Source: Dext, supplier portals, bank feeds.
  2. Code & post transactions — assign each item to a COA account and a VAT code; post to the ledger.
  3. Reconcile the bank — match every line on the bank statement to a posting in the bank ledger; investigate unmatched items.
  4. Reconcile control accounts — debtors control to AR ageing; creditors control to AP ageing; VAT control to VAT return draft; PAYE control to payroll summary.
  5. Adjusting journals — accruals, prepayments, depreciation, stock movement, payroll accruals, deferred income.
  6. Run a trial balance — confirm Dr=Cr; review for unusual balances (negative assets, etc.).
  7. Run management accounts — P&L vs budget, balance sheet snapshot, key KPIs.
  8. Lock the period — set lock date in software so prior-period postings are blocked.
  9. File & archive — store source documents, save management pack, log version.

5.2 Bank reconciliation in depth

A bank reconciliation answers: does my cash book agree with the bank's record of my cash? The starting point is the bank statement closing balance. From it, you adjust for unpresented cheques, uncleared lodgements and bank-only items (charges, interest, direct debits not yet recorded), to arrive at the cash book balance. In the cloud-feed era this is largely automated, but the discipline must be understood.

6.4 MTD digital records rules

Under MTD for VAT (and from 2026 onwards, MTD ITSA), records must be kept “digitally” and there must be a “digital link” between every step from source to filed return. Manual re-typing into a spreadsheet between two systems breaks the digital link and is non-compliant. Permitted digital links include:

  • API connections (the gold standard — Xero/QBO/etc. talking directly).
  • CSV import/export between systems.
  • Email of XLS/XLSX files (digital files), where the data is then imported (not retyped).
  • Linked cells within a spreadsheet (cell A1 in sheet 1 = A1 in sheet 2).

Not permitted: rekeying numbers; copy-paste of values (formula loss); transcribing a paper printout into a spreadsheet.

Cash vs Accruals Basis

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish cash basis from accruals (traditional) basis.
  • Identify which businesses must use accruals and which may use cash.
  • Apply the transition adjustments when moving from cash to accruals (or vice versa).

7.1 The two bases defined

Cash basis: income is recognised when cash is received; expenses are recognised when paid. No debtors, creditors, accruals or prepayments. Easier for the smallest businesses.

Accruals (traditional) basis: income is recognised when earned (invoice raised); expenses recognised when incurred (invoice received), regardless of cash flow. The standard required by FRS 102 / FRS 105 for company accounts and the default for income tax purposes for businesses above the cash basis threshold.

7.2 Cash basis — eligibility (FA 2013, ITTOIA 2005 s.25A onwards)

Until 5 April 2024 the cash basis was elective for sole traders and partnerships with turnover up to £150,000 (must leave at £300,000). From 6 April 2024 the cash basis is the default for all unincorporated businesses; you must elect out if you want accruals. Turnover thresholds were also abolished. This is a major change brought in by the Spring Budget 2024.

Cash basis is not available to: limited companies (must use accruals under FRS); LLPs; partnerships with a corporate partner; farms using herd basis; ministers of religion; managed service companies; Lloyd's underwriters.

7.4 Transition adjustments

When moving from cash to accruals (or accruals to cash), a transition adjustment is required to avoid double-counting or non-counting of income and expenses across the changeover (ITTOIA 2005 ss.232–240).

Worked Example 7.1

Sole trader switching from cash to accruals

Year 1 (cash): Sales received £80,000. Year 2 (accruals): At end of Year 1 there were £8,000 of unpaid sales invoices and £3,000 of unpaid supplier invoices.

Transition adjustment in Year 2:

  • Add £8,000 to income (debtors created on transition date — would never have been counted under cash basis).
  • Add £3,000 to expenses (creditors created on transition date).

Net adjustment: +£5,000 added to taxable profit in Year 2 — spread over 6 years if on transition to MTD ITSA.

Module 7 — Summary

  • Cash basis is now the default for unincorporated UK businesses (from 2024/25).
  • Limited companies must use accruals (FRS 102/105 mandate).
  • Transition adjustments under ITTOIA 2005 prevent double-counting on basis change.

VAT Fundamentals — Registration, Rates, Tax Points & Returns

Learning Objectives

  • State the four VAT rates in the UK and identify supplies that fall in each.
  • Apply the registration thresholds (compulsory and voluntary) and identify the trigger for backwards/forwards registration.
  • Determine the tax point of any supply.
  • Complete a 9-box VAT return from a trial balance.
  • Calculate output and input VAT for any standard scenario.

13.1 What is VAT, conceptually?

VAT is a multi-stage transaction tax on the value added at each link of a supply chain. Each VAT-registered business charges output VAT on its sales, recovers input VAT on its purchases, and pays the net to HMRC. The economic burden falls on the final non-VAT-registered consumer; intermediate businesses are tax collectors, not taxpayers, on each transaction.

Legislative basis: Value Added Tax Act 1994 (VATA 1994); secondary legislation in the VAT Regulations 1995 (SI 1995/2518); HMRC public notices (Notice 700 series) provide interpretation but are not law.

13.2 The four UK VAT rates

RateDescriptionExamples
20% standardDefault rate for almost all suppliesMost goods and services; professional services; restaurant meals; clothing for adults; alcohol
5% reducedSpecific listed supplies (VATA Sch 7A)Domestic gas/electricity; energy-saving materials; children's car seats; certain conversions of dwellings
0% zero-ratedListed supplies (VATA Sch 8) — taxable, just at 0%Most food (not catering); books, journals, e-books; children's clothing & footwear; new-build residential construction; passenger transport (10+ seat vehicles); newspapers & magazines
ExemptListed supplies (VATA Sch 9) — outside the scope of VAT charge but block input recoveryInsurance; finance (most); education; health & welfare; postal services; betting & gaming; sale of land/buildings unless opted
Zero-rated vs Exempt — the critical difference

Both result in £0 VAT being charged on the sale — but the consequence for input VAT recovery differs. Zero-rated sales are taxable supplies; the supplier can fully recover input VAT on costs incurred to make them. Exempt sales are non-taxable supplies; input VAT on costs to make exempt supplies is blocked (subject to partial-exemption rules — Module 18). A VAT-registered insurance broker recovers no input VAT on its overheads; a VAT-registered children's clothing retailer recovers all of it.

13.2.1 Out of scope (OoS)

A separate concept: the supply isn't made in the UK at all (place of supply is overseas), or it isn't a supply for VAT purposes (wages, dividends, statutory fines). OoS items don't appear in Box 6 of the VAT return at all (in most cases — cross-border services to UK businesses can be reverse-charged into Box 6).

13.3 Registration

13.3.1 Compulsory registration thresholds (2024/25)

£90,000 per rolling 12 months of taxable turnover (raised from £85,000 on 1 April 2024). Two tests:

  1. Historic test — at the end of any month, taxable supplies in the prior 12 months exceed £90,000. Must register within 30 days; effective date is the first day of the month after the breach.
  2. Future test — you reasonably expect taxable turnover in the next 30 days alone to exceed £90,000. Effective date is the start of that 30-day period.

13.3.2 Deregistration threshold

£88,000 (raised from £83,000 on 1 April 2024). Voluntary deregistration available when taxable turnover in next 12 months is reasonably expected to be below £88,000.

13.3.3 Voluntary registration

Available regardless of turnover, including for businesses making zero-rated supplies (often beneficial — can recover input VAT). Not available for businesses making only exempt supplies.

Worked Example 13.1 — Registration date

A consultancy crosses £90,000 cumulative taxable turnover on 18 May 2025 (a single big invoice tipped it over). What is the VAT registration deadline and effective date?

  • Threshold breached at end of May 2025 (historic test).
  • Must notify HMRC by 30 June 2025 (within 30 days of end of breach month).
  • Effective date of registration: 1 July 2025 (first day of second month after breach).
  • From 1 July 2025, must charge output VAT on all standard-rated sales.

For goods or services where invoice is issued within 14 days of the basic tax point, the actual tax point becomes the invoice date. VAT Schemes — Standard, Cash, Annual, Flat Rate, Margin, TOMS, Retail

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish the standard scheme from the alternative schemes.
  • Apply eligibility tests for each.
  • Calculate VAT under Flat Rate, Cash, Annual, Margin, TOMS and Retail schemes.
  • Recommend the correct scheme for a given client profile.

14.1 Standard accounting (accruals) scheme

Default scheme. Output VAT accounted for at the tax point of the sale (typically invoice date); input VAT recovered at the tax point of the purchase. Means VAT can be due on a sales invoice before the customer has paid — cashflow burden for businesses with long debtor days.

14.2 Cash Accounting Scheme

Output VAT due when payment received; input VAT recoverable when paid. Solves the cashflow issue. Eligibility: taxable turnover ≤ £1.35m (entry); leave when > £1.6m. Available to most but not for: imports, intra-NI/EU acquisitions, supplies more than 6 months overdue from supplier.

14.3 Annual Accounting Scheme

One annual return + 9 monthly (or 3 quarterly) interim payments based on prior-year liability. Final balancing payment with the return. Same £1.35m / £1.6m thresholds. Combine with Cash Accounting allowed.

14.4 Flat Rate Scheme (FRS)

You charge customers VAT at standard rates but pay HMRC a fixed flat-rate percentage of your VAT-inclusive turnover. You generally cannot reclaim input VAT (except on capital assets >£2,000 single transaction including VAT).

Entry: turnover (excl. VAT) ≤ £150k in next 12 months. Exit: VAT-inclusive turnover > £230k. First-year discount: 1% off published rate.

14.4.1 Limited Cost Trader (LCT) — anti-abuse rule

If goods cost <2% of VAT-inclusive turnover (or <£1,000/yr), the business is an LCT and pays a punitive 16.5% flat rate. Effectively eliminates FRS benefit for labour-only consultancies.

Worked Example 14.1 — FRS LCT

IT consultant, turnover £80,000+VAT (£96,000 incl.). Goods spend £200/year. LCT applies (200 < 2% of 96,000 = 1,920).

14.5 Margin Schemes

For dealers in second-hand goods, antiques, art, used cars. VAT calculated on the dealer's margin (sale − purchase price). Input VAT on the goods cannot be recovered.

14.6 TOMS — Tour Operators' Margin Scheme

Mandatory for businesses buying in and reselling travel/accommodation/passenger transport bundled as a package. VAT on the margin. Specialist sector.

14.7 Retail Schemes

For shops with mostly cash B2C sales. Variants: Point-of-sale (modern EPOS), Apportionment (mixed-rate till takings split), Direct Calculation. Allow accounting without per-transaction VAT invoices.

14.8 VAT Group Registration (VATA s.43)

Bodies corporate under common control may form a single VAT group (one VRN, one return). Internal supplies disregarded. Joint and several liability among members. Care needed with partially-exempt members.

  • Cash Accounting (≤£1.35m) helps cashflow when customers slow-pay.
  • Annual Accounting reduces admin — 1 return + interim payments.
  • FRS can save VAT but Limited Cost Trader rules block the benefit for labour-only businesses.
  • Margin, TOMS, Retail schemes for specialised sectors.
  • Group registration for commonly-controlled bodies corporate.

Partial Exemption

Learning Objectives

  • Identify a partially-exempt business.
  • Apply the Standard Method.
  • Apply the de minimis tests.
  • Perform the Annual Adjustment.

18.1 Partial-exemption principle

A business making both taxable and exempt supplies cannot recover all input VAT. Direct attribution + residual apportionment.

18.2 The Standard Method (Reg 101)

  1. Direct attribution: assign each input to taxable / exempt / residual.
  2. Recovery percentage on residual = Taxable supplies (excl. VAT) / Total supplies (excl. VAT) × 100, rounded UP to nearest whole percent (unless residual VAT > £400k/month).
  3. Recover: directly-attributable taxable + (residual × recovery%).

18.3 De minimis

If total exempt input VAT (direct + share of residual) is below de minimis, all exempt input VAT becomes recoverable. Test: exempt input VAT ≤ £625/month average AND ≤ 50% of total input VAT.

18.4 Annual Adjustment

Quarterly recovery uses provisional ratios; annual adjustment recalculates using full-year figures and adjusts in the next return after year-end.

Worked Example 18.1 — PE calculation

Period: taxable supplies £400k; exempt supplies £100k. Input VAT: directly taxable £30k; directly exempt £5k; residual £20k.

Recovery% = 400 / 500 = 80%.

Recovery = £30k + £20k × 80% = £46k. Blocked = £5k + £4k = £9k. De minimis: £9k > £625 × 3 months = £1,875 → fails. £9k blocked.

Module 18 — Summary

  • Direct attribution + residual apportionment by taxable/total.
  • De minimis allows full recovery if exempt input VAT is small.
  • Annual adjustment reconciles year's quarterly provisionals.

Source: FRS 102 s.33. Why: Non-arm's-length transactions can transfer value to a related party. Disclosure deters and exposes.

Source: FRS 102 s.3.3 + CA 2006 s.474. Why: Tells users which framework governs the figures so they know which R&M rules apply.

Source: FRS 102 s.3.8. Why: Affirmation that accounts on going-concern basis. Material uncertainty disclosed.

Note 6: Operating Profit (Charges)

38.1 The new schemes (from 1 April 2024)

Old SME and RDEC schemes merged. Two routes now:

39.2 Loss Relief Restriction (LRR) — c/f losses

From 1 April 2017:

  • £5m Deductions Allowance per group, used freely
  • Losses above allowance restricted to 50% of remaining profit

So £20m profit + £20m b/f losses = offset £5m + 50%×£15m = £12.5m → £7.5m taxable.

Parent owns ≥75% of subsidiary's ordinary share capital, ≥75% beneficial entitlement to distributable profits and assets on winding-up. Losses surrendered between members applied against overlapping AP.

  • Loan relationship deficit: similar 5 reliefs but ring-fenced.

39.5 Anti-avoidance

CTA 2010 s.673 — losses denied if major change in nature of trade within 5 years of change in ownership. Prevents “loss buying”.

Choosing a Structure — The Decision Framework

49.1 The five key questions

  1. Liability: how much could the business lose to creditors? Higher = limited structure.

Module 55 · Part 8 of 12 · ~3 hours

Sole Trader → Ltd Company Incorporation

The most common transition. Done badly: triggers CGT on goodwill, leaves stranded VAT, creates a tax-inefficient extraction structure for years. Done well: preserves losses, secures BADR eligibility, creates tax-efficient extraction from day 1.

55.1 The 9-step process

  1. Form the new Ltd (Module 52).
  2. Value the business assets: tangible (book value), inventory (cost), debtors (face value), goodwill (see 55.2).
  3. Sale agreement: written contract sole trader → Ltd transferring assets and goodwill.
  4. VAT TOGC: if VAT-registered, structure as TOGC (Module 17) to avoid VAT on transfer.
  5. CGT: s.162 incorporation relief: where consideration is wholly shares, s.162 applies automatically to defer entire gain on goodwill into share base cost. Mixed consideration → partial s.162 (relief proportional to share value / total consideration).
  6. HMRC notify cessation: file final SA103 marking cessation.
  7. VAT: VAT68 to transfer VRN to Ltd, OR sole trader deregisters and Ltd applies for new VRN.
  8. Bank, contracts, insurance, suppliers: novate or reissue in Ltd's name. Some (commercial leases, finance) require landlord/lender consent.
  9. Open BS of Ltd: post asset transfer journal.

55.2 Goodwill — the technically tricky bit

Goodwill = value beyond identifiable assets. On incorporation, technically a CGT disposal at MV (s.18 TCGA 1992 — connected-party at MV).

Two main treatments:

  • Pure share consideration → s.162 incorporation relief: gain rolled into share base cost; no immediate CGT. Required: all assets transferred to Ltd in exchange for shares.
  • Mixed (shares + cash/DLA): s.162 partial (£ shares ÷ £ total × gain). The cash/DLA portion of gain is taxable now but BADR may apply (10% up to £1m lifetime) provided 2-year qualifying trade.

55.3 Goodwill amortisation in Ltd

Acquired goodwill amortised under FRS 102 s.18/s.19 (presumed 10-year life). However goodwill amortisation generally non-deductible for CT since 2002 (FA 2002 reform). From April 2019 narrow corporate intangibles regime allows 6.5% write-down where goodwill acquired alongside “qualifying IP” — most plain incorporations don't qualify.

55.4 Other transfer considerations

  • Trading stock: transfer at higher of cost or MV (TCGA s.161). MV creates P&L credit in sole trader's final accounts.
  • Plant & machinery: usually elect TWDV transfer to avoid balancing charge/allowance (CAA 2001 s.266).
  • Property: SDLT applies on land at MV between connected parties (FA 2003 s.53). Often property left in personal name and licensed to Ltd to avoid SDLT.
  • VAT TOGC: meet conditions or face VAT on entire asset transfer.
  • DLA: if Ltd buys assets but doesn't pay cash, the unpaid balance is a credit to Director's Loan — drawable tax-free as repayment of loan in future.

55.5 Loss treatment on transition

  • Sole trader losses: carried forward to set against future income from same trade — but the trade has ceased! Limited use; can elect under s.86 ITA 2007 to relieve against income from new Ltd only if consideration ≥80% shares.
  • Capital losses: remain personal; no transfer.

55.6 Practitioner's incorporation checklist

  1. “Is incorporation worth it” tax model on actual numbers?
  2. 2 years' trade for BADR eligibility?
  3. Goodwill valuation defensible?
  4. VAT TOGC structuring confirmed?
  5. Property treatment decided?
  6. Sale agreement drafted/signed?
  7. Final sole trader SA filed (cessation noted)?
  8. New Ltd opening BS posted?
  9. DLA / share base cost recorded?

Module 55 — Summary

  • Sole trader → Ltd is a CGT event on goodwill at MV (s.18 TCGA).
  • s.162 defers gain if wholly shares; partial if mixed.
  • BADR may cover taxable portion at 10% (2-year qualifying period).
  • TOGC for VAT; elect P&M at TWDV; consider leaving property in personal name.
  • Goodwill amortisation in Ltd usually non-deductible.
  • Final sole trader SA marks cessation.

Administration & CVA

64.1 Administration (IA 1986 Sch B1)

  • Court protection while administrator restructures or sells as going concern
  • Moratorium on creditor action
  • 3 statutory objectives (in order): rescue going concern; better creditor result; realise assets to pay secured/preferential
  • 12-month duration (extendable)
  • Pre-pack sale (sale to connected party arranged before administration) — controversial

64.2 CVA (IA 1986 Part I)

  • Compromise with creditors — pay reduced amounts over time
  • 75% creditor approval (by value)

  • Simplification — owner tired of CT, CH filings
  • Profits dropped below break-even (Module 49)
  • Cost of compliance exceeds tax saving
  • Owner doesn't need limited liability

67.2 No clean tax route

Unlike sole trader → Ltd (s.162 incorporation relief), there is no equivalent reverse relief. Disincorporation Relief (2013–2018) expired.

Ltd transferring business to director triggers:

  • CGT on goodwill at MV (s.18 TCGA — connected parties)
  • Balancing CA charge on P&M
  • Stock at MV
  • VAT issues (TOGC may apply)
  • Distribution to shareholder (capital if MVL, dividend if direct)

  1. Value business assets and goodwill.
  2. Decide route: MVL (capital + BADR) or wind-down + DS01 (capital ≤£25k).

  6. Final CT600 covers period to cessation.
  7. Distribution to shareholder.
  8. CH closure (DS01 or post-MVL dissolution).
  9. Sole trader operates from new VRN (or transferred VRN).

Many owners either: (a) leave Ltd dormant rather than disincorporate; or (b) MVL for capital extraction at BADR, then continue as sole trader if TAAR doesn't apply.

IR35 / Off-Payroll Working Rules

75.1 What IR35 is

Anti-avoidance: prevents individuals avoiding employee taxes by interposing a personal service company (PSC). If the engagement “would be employment” absent the PSC, IR35 applies — full PAYE/NI on deemed employment income.

75.2 Status tests (Ready Mixed Concrete v MoP 1968)

  1. Personal Service: must the worker do the work, or can they substitute?
  2. Mutuality of Obligation (MOO): ongoing offer/accept obligation?
  3. Control: how, what, when, where over the work?

Plus secondary: financial risk, equipment, exclusivity, integration, intention.

75.3 Who decides status

  • Pre-April 2017 private sector: worker (via PSC).
  • Public sector since April 2017: client (SDS — Status Determination Statement).
  • Private sector since April 2021: medium/large clients determine; small clients = worker still decides.

75.4 If inside IR35

  • Fee payer (client/agency) deducts PAYE/NI on deemed direct payment to worker
  • Employer NI applies (fee payer = NI employer)
  • Worker offsets deemed employment income against PSC income to avoid double tax

75.5 CEST

HMRC's status check tool. HMRC stands by results if answers honest. Many disputes; tribunals have overturned.

Module 75 — Summary

  • IR35 prevents disguised employment via PSCs.
  • Tests: Personal service, MOO, Control + secondary.
  • Determination: medium/large clients in private sector since April 2021.
  • Inside IR35 → fee payer deducts PAYE/NI.

GDPR for Accountants

86.1 Status

Most practices = data controller for client data. Outsourced functions (payroll bureau) → data processor.

86.2 Six lawful bases

  1. Consent
  2. Contract performance
  3. Legal obligation
  4. Vital interests
  5. Public task
  6. Legitimate interests

  • Privacy notice on website

  • DPIAs for high-risk processing
  • Personal data breach reporting to ICO within 72 hours
  • Data subject rights: access, rectification, erasure, portability, restriction, objection

Up to £17.5m or 4% global turnover (greater).

Professional Indemnity Insurance (PII)

87.1 Why PII

Required by professional bodies. Covers negligence, errors, breach of duty.

87.2 Cover (ICAEW)

  • Minimum: 2.5× gross fee income; capped £1.5m for fees ≤£600k
  • Minimum £100k for smallest practices
  • Aggregate cap typically 1× cover
  • Excess: £1k–£10k

87.3 What PII covers

  • Civil claims for damages
  • Defence costs
  • Loss of client documents
  • Some include cyber and run-off

87.4 Run-off cover

6 years post-cessation (matches Limitation Act period). Significant cost — budget into closure plan.
